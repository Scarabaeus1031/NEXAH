# NEXAH Navigation Primitives

This document defines the first operational vocabulary for **navigation in NEXAH**.

It does not yet present a complete navigation engine.

Its purpose is more fundamental:

> to define the basic entities, transitions, and signals that make navigation in NEXAH possible.

At the current stage of the project, NEXAH is strongest in:

- structure discovery
- field discovery
- transition geometry
- early instability visibility

Navigation is therefore understood here not as a finished control system, but as an **emerging layer built on top of extracted structure**.

---

## 1. What navigation means in NEXAH

In NEXAH, navigation is **not** primarily:

- shortest-path search
- reward optimization
- blind control
- arbitrary steering through state space

Instead, navigation means:

> movement through a structured stability field in a way that remains coherent with the geometry of the system.

This implies that navigation depends on:

- detectable structure
- readable field direction
- identifiable transition zones
- stable or semi-stable corridors
- signals of collapse, inversion, or loss of coherence

In this sense, NEXAH navigation is best understood as:

~~~text
coherence-guided movement through structured dynamical geometry
~~~

## 2. Canonical navigation spaces

NEXAH can be explored through different types of navigation spaces.

These spaces do not all have the same status, but they contribute to a common navigational logic.

### A. Regime Landscapes

These are state- and transition-based spaces in which:

- regimes appear
- basin structure becomes visible
- transition regions can be localized
- risk geometry can be mapped

This is the classical NEXAH architecture layer.

---

### B. Field Spaces

These are spaces in which system dynamics are represented as:

- directional flow
- local drift
- branching structure
- coherent or incoherent motion

This is where navigation begins to move beyond state transitions and into geometry.

---

### C. Recursive Navigation Spaces

Fractal and recursive systems such as Mandelbrot-like spaces can serve as **canonical navigation models** for NEXAH.

These spaces reveal:

- recursive basin logic
- nested stability / instability
- filament corridors
- branch proliferation
- zoom-dependent transition structure

These are important because they show that navigation may occur not only in graphs or state sets, but in **recursive structural landscapes**.

---

### D. Proto-Geometric Spaces

Exploratory models such as:

- oval membrane fields
- time knot fields
- core / ring systems
- mirror spaces
- inside-out pearl structures

represent proto-geometric navigation spaces.

These are still under development, but they may later provide the deeper syntax of NEXAH navigation.

## 3. Navigation primitives

The following primitives form the first vocabulary of navigation in NEXAH.

They should be understood as **structural roles**, not necessarily as fixed visual shapes.

---

### 3.1 Core

The **core** is the reference center of a navigational structure.

It may represent:

- a stability anchor
- a singularity-like center
- a local invariance region
- a coherence kernel
- a measurement center

A core is not always safe, but it is always structurally important.

Questions:

- Is the system centered on the core?
- Is the core stable, overloaded, split, or collapsing?
- Is the core acting as an anchor or as a singularity trap?

---

### 3.2 Ring

A **ring** is a recurrent structural shell around a center or reference axis.

A ring may represent:

- a recurrence band
- an orbital stability zone
- a shell of allowable motion
- a threshold boundary
- a nested transition level

Questions:

- Which ring is the system currently occupying?
- Is the system moving inward, outward, or along the ring?
- Is the next ring reachable or blocked?

---

### 3.3 Plane

A **plane** is a structural projection surface or interpretive domain.

Examples may include:

- Mandelbrot plane
- Julia plane
- exponent / X-plane
- field slice
- phase plane
- control plane

A plane is useful because it stabilizes one aspect of system behavior into a readable form.

Questions:

- Which plane best reveals the current structure?
- Is the system staying on one plane or crossing between planes?
- Are multiple planes coupled or decoupled?

---

### 3.4 Gate

A **gate** is a localized transition opening between regions, rings, planes, or modes.

A gate may represent:

- a threshold crossing
- an allowable entry point
- a resonance opening
- a narrow transition corridor
- a critical passage

Questions:

- Is the gate open, closed, unstable, or one-way?
- What signal indicates gate accessibility?
- What is the cost of crossing?

---

### 3.5 Branch

A **branch** is a directional split in the evolution of the system.

A branch may represent:

- divergence after transition
- bifurcation pathway
- collapse branch
- recovery branch
- choice structure in the field

Questions:

- Is the system entering a branch point?
- Which branch preserves coherence?
- Which branch leads to instability?

---

### 3.6 Corridor

A **corridor** is a region of relatively coherent movement through the system.

A corridor may represent:

- a stable route
- a low-risk transition channel
- a field-aligned trajectory band
- a path of delayed collapse
- a temporary navigation passage

Questions:

- Is the corridor persistent or shrinking?
- Is it globally stable or only locally stable?
- Is it recoverable after partial deviation?

---

### 3.7 Mirror

A **mirror** is a reflection relation between regions, layers, or spaces.

A mirror may represent:

- symmetry relation
- inside-out inversion
- reflected branch logic
- dual view across an interface
- paired structural correspondence

A mirror is important because it may preserve form while reversing direction or interpretation.

Questions:

- Is the mirror conservative or distorting?
- What is preserved across reflection?
- Does the mirror reverse flow orientation?

---

### 3.8 Clamp

A **clamp** is a local structural constraint that holds or fixes a relation.

A clamp may represent:

- a lock
- a boundary fixation
- a phase constraint
- a coupling anchor
- a forced alignment

Questions:

- Is the system freely moving or clamped?
- Is the clamp stabilizing or overconstraining?
- Can the clamp be released without collapse?

---

### 3.9 Split

A **split** is the separation of a previously unified structure into distinct directional or functional sectors.

A split may represent:

- coherence split
- life/death split
- red/blue or forward/backward split
- phase separation
- stability / instability partition

Questions:

- What exactly is splitting?
- Is the split reversible?
- Does navigation require crossing or respecting the split?

---

### 3.10 Interface

An **interface** is the boundary region where different structural domains meet.

It may be:

- a coherence boundary
- a transition manifold
- a temporal knot
- a measurement zone
- a phase boundary

The interface is especially important in NEXAH because many decisive changes happen there.

Questions:

- Is the interface crossing smooth or singular?
- Does time emerge or become measurable there?
- Is the interface passable or critical?

---

## 4. Allowed transitions

Navigation requires not just entities, but rules for moving between them.

The following transition classes are currently the most important.

### A. Core ↔ Ring

Movement between center and shell.

Examples:

- stabilization toward the core
- escape from core overload
- outward shell transition

---

### B. Ring ↔ Ring

Movement between recurrence shells.

Examples:

- expansion
- contraction
- resonance shift
- next-layer activation

---

### C. Plane ↔ Plane

Movement between different projection domains.

Examples:

- Mandelbrot ↔ Julia
- phase ↔ field
- measurement ↔ geometry
- symbolic ↔ operational plane

This is crucial for NEXAH because not all structures are visible on a single plane.

---

### D. Corridor ↔ Branch

Movement from guided coherence into decision structure.

Examples:

- corridor collapse into branch
- branch selection from coherent drift
- corridor recovery after branch deviation

---

### E. Mirror Crossing

Movement across a reflected or inverted domain.

Examples:

- inside-out mapping
- dual branch inversion
- breath mirror transition
- lower / upper realm reflection

---

### F. Interface Crossing

Movement across critical boundaries.

Examples:

- coherence threshold crossing
- transition from forward field to backward field
- temporal knot activation
- instability onset

---

## 5. Navigation signals

The primitives above become operational only when paired with measurable or interpretable signals.

These signals do not yet form a complete calculus, but they define what NEXAH should read during navigation.

### 5.1 Coherence

The most important signal.

Coherence measures whether motion remains aligned with the structural field.

Possible interpretation:

- high coherence → stable movement
- low coherence → transition zone
- negative coherence → inversion / opposing flow

---

### 5.2 Drift

Drift indicates the current directional tendency of the system.

Questions:

- Is the system staying on track?
- Is it slowly leaving a corridor?
- Is drift accelerating toward collapse?

---

### 5.3 Collapse tendency

A signal that the current motion is approaching structural failure.

Examples:

- loss of corridor coherence
- branch divergence toward instability
- unreturnable interface crossing

---

### 5.4 Return tendency

A signal that the system still has recoverable alignment.

Examples:

- re-entry into forward field
- ring re-stabilization
- return from mirror distortion
- partial re-coherence after transition

---

### 5.5 Resonance alignment

A signal that the system is moving through a structurally meaningful channel.

Examples:

- shell locking
- ring compatibility
- frequency correspondence
- phase corridor alignment
