# Baseline Summary

Photosynthesis is a group of biological processes through which organisms containing light-absorbing pigments convert light energy into chemical energy. Plants, algae, and cyanobacteria are familiar examples. In oxygenic photosynthesis, water supplies electrons and oxygen is released as a byproduct. Other organisms use forms of photosynthesis that do not release oxygen or fix carbon in the same way, so the familiar plant process is important but not the only version.

In plants and algae, photosynthesis mainly occurs in chloroplasts. Within them, thylakoid membranes contain pigments such as chlorophyll and the protein complexes that capture light. The process is often introduced in two linked stages. During the light-dependent reactions, absorbed light drives electron transfer, water splitting, and the formation of ATP and NADPH. These molecules carry usable energy and reducing power. During the light-independent reactions, including the Calvin cycle, ATP and NADPH help incorporate carbon dioxide into organic compounds that can contribute to carbohydrates. Photosynthesis and cellular respiration are related in the movement of energy and carbon, but they are distinct processes with different reaction sequences and cellular locations.

The article also places photosynthesis at wider scales. Photosynthetic organisms supply much of the biological energy used by complex life and are central to atmospheric oxygen and global carbon conversion. The process has a long evolutionary history: early photosynthesis may have been anoxygenic, while oxygenic photosynthesis later transformed Earth’s atmosphere and helped make complex life possible. Chloroplasts are understood through endosymbiotic history, linking present plant cells to photosynthetic bacteria.

Photosynthetic performance is limited by interacting conditions rather than a single input. Light intensity and wavelength, water, carbon-dioxide concentration, temperature, leaf area, anatomy, and photorespiration can all matter. C3, C4, and other carbon-concentrating strategies respond differently to these constraints; several major crops are C4 plants. Researchers measure light and carbon-assimilation reactions separately or together and investigate ways to increase plant yield. The article therefore presents photosynthesis not only as a chemical mechanism, but also as a cellular, organismal, ecological, evolutionary, and agricultural subject.
