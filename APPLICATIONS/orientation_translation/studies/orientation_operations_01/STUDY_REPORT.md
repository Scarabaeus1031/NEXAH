# ORIENTATION OPERATIONS
## Exploratory Review 01

## Objective

Determine whether the thirteen recurring editorial operations admitted by
[Editorial Grammar of Orientation 01](../editorial_grammar_of_orientation_01/STUDY_REPORT.md)
consistently support distinct orientation functions that survive changes of
layout, style, representation, and domain.

## Input boundary

The previous study is the primary and fixed analytical input. Its admitted
operations are treated as observational data and are not redefined or
renamed. Source IDs `C-01` through `C-26` refer to its
[Corpus Register](../editorial_grammar_of_orientation_01/CORPUS_REGISTER.md).

This review examines artifact-supported function. It does not observe readers,
infer cognition, or establish psychological, biological, or universal
operations.

## Method

For each admitted operation the review records:

1. its unchanged editorial description;
2. what task appears easier in the reviewed artifacts;
3. whether necessity is demonstrated;
4. a case in which orientation can occur without it;
5. the failure pressure documented when it is absent;
6. evidence strength;
7. survival across changes of layout, color, icon, language, work, and domain.

Dependencies, failure modes, domain variation, and independence from visual
realization are assessed separately. Similar functions are not merged.

## Major findings

### Distinct functions recur

All thirteen operations support identifiable artifact-level orientation
functions with strong or moderate evidence across more than one artifact class.
Four—including juxtaposition, typed sequencing, stopping, and reopening through
questions—remain moderate because their meaning depends more strongly on
labels, context, or a narrower set of artifact purposes.

The recurring functions include:

- distinguishing kinds of representation;
- making comparison possible without identity collapse;
- tracking a declared order or relation;
- entering through different questions;
- locating differences;
- checking limits, evidence, and provenance;
- preventing scope and scale leakage;
- preserving alternatives and directional asymmetry;
- recognizing local closure and unresolved continuation.

### Function survives style more often than form

Separation, comparison, qualification, evidence labeling, boundary marking,
scope declaration, traceability, and alternative preservation appear in
tables, prose, diagrams, matrices, paths, and repository navigation. They do
not require a particular palette, icon set, or panel geometry.

Sequencing and juxtaposition survive style changes but depend more strongly on
explicit relation labels. An arrow or spatial adjacency alone does not preserve
their function.

### Necessity is not demonstrated

No operation is shown to be universally mandatory. Artifacts can orient at a
local level without alternative routes, stopping statements, or open questions.
They become more vulnerable to specific documented failures, but absence does
not prove disorientation.

### Dependencies are conditional

Some operations enable or strengthen others, but no universal hierarchy is
supported. Comparison usually benefits from separated objects and a declared
basis; stopping benefits from scope and boundary marking; verification benefits
from traceability and evidence labels. These are functional supports, not a
mandatory execution order.

## Quantitative summary

- operations assessed: 13
- recurring orientation functions supported: 13
- strong evidence: 9
- moderate evidence: 4
- operations demonstrated necessary: 0
- candidate universal cognitive operations: 0

## Limitations

The full uncertainty register is in [LIMITATIONS.md](LIMITATIONS.md). The
strongest limitation is unchanged from the previous study: function is inferred
from artifact structure and explicit editorial claims, not measured through
independent reader behavior. Shared authorship and templates also limit
independence.

## Final disposition

# RECURRING ORIENTATION FUNCTIONS

Several recurring editorial operations consistently support identifiable
orientation functions across multiple domains and representation types while
remaining independent of specific visual styles. This does not make them
universal, mandatory, cognitive, canonical, or formal.
