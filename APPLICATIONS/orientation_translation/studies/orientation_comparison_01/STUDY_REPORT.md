# HOW ORIENTATION IS COMPARED

## Study 01 — Preparing Cross-Neighborhood Comparison

## Scope

This study designs a future comparison discipline. It does not construct a second pilot, compare two subjects, modify a neighborhood, build an Atlas, define an ontology, create OH-001, or change NEXAH architecture.

Its research question is:

> How can two Orientation Neighborhoods be compared without destroying their independent evidence boundaries?

The answer is procedural: freeze both neighborhoods, compare referenced records one dimension at a time, preserve every difference and uncertainty, and store the result outside both inputs.

## 1. What is a comparison object?

A comparison object is a frozen, addressable record with inspectable provenance and local meaning. Eligible objects include the whole neighborhood, nodes, directional edges, roles, support classes, stopping boundaries, and rejections. Reader paths, lenses, bridges, and growth events are conditional objects because they are comparable only when both neighborhoods explicitly record them.

Labels, colors, icons, visual position, topology, and raw counts are not sufficient comparison objects. They may nominate a question but cannot establish a result.

## 2. What may legitimately be compared?

Legitimate dimensions include identity, direction, evidence, role, scale, function, local depth, stopping behavior, rejection, bridge structure, hidden questions, and growth events. Each dimension has its own evidence and outcome.

A comparison may report equivalence in one dimension, compatibility, difference, incomparability, or unresolved status. A positive result in one dimension cannot settle another. Identity does not establish role equivalence; shared role does not establish identity; matching topology does not establish meaning.

## 3. What may never be assumed?

The framework prohibits assumptions of:

- shared identity from a shared label;
- shared meaning from shared structure;
- equal evidence from equal support labels;
- reciprocity from one directional edge;
- transitivity from adjacent relations;
- shared role from shared identity;
- shared identity from shared role;
- completeness from node or edge count;
- Atlas eligibility from vocabulary, diagrams, visual similarity, topology, or template alignment;
- absence where only non-recording is known.

No comparison may mutate, merge, normalize, or upgrade its inputs.

## 4. How is identity established?

Identity is tested through referent, definition, scope, scale, source, and version. Same label, function, role, position, process, or scale is insufficient alone. The strongest valid result is a reviewed `same_scoped_referent` correspondence.

Even that result does not merge nodes. Both local records retain their roles, edges, evidence, rejections, and histories. Partial overlap, functional analogy, same-label/different-referent, difference, and unresolved identity remain explicit alternatives.

## 5. How is reciprocity tested?

A → B and B → A are audited as two independent directional claims. Each requires its own relation semantics, support, scope, uncertainty, and unsupported implications. Only when both directions are independently supported and compatible may the comparison record `reciprocal_supported`.

Two supported directions can still be `bidirectional_different` when their meanings or scopes differ. One-way support, editorial reverse navigation, apparent reciprocity, incomparability, and unresolved status are valid outcomes.

## 6. Why is asymmetry important?

Asymmetry preserves information that symmetry-driven comparison would erase. Differences in structure, identity, direction, evidence, role, scale, rejection, chronology, and interpretation may locate the most significant boundaries between neighborhoods.

Asymmetry is not automatically error, conflict, or discovery. It becomes useful when the compared dimension, evidence, uncertainty, and prohibited inference are recorded. A valid framework must therefore be able to conclude that two neighborhoods differ meaningfully without ranking or repairing them.

## 7. When may an Atlas begin?

Atlas consideration may begin only after each neighborhood has immutable identity, provenance, inspectable relations, support and uncertainty records, preserved rejections, stopping rationale, and independent construction history. A pairwise comparison must then establish a bounded question, reviewed identity candidates, directional and reciprocity results, preserved support inequalities, scale and scope handling, asymmetry records, failure audit, and human approval.

This establishes eligibility for a later review only. It neither constructs nor authorizes an Atlas. Shared vocabulary, diagrams, visual similarity, counts, topology, domain, or template are expressly insufficient.

## 8. What remains unknown?

The framework has not been executed. Unknowns include:

- whether independent reviewers apply the tests consistently;
- whether identity and support records are sufficiently granular;
- how often useful comparisons end as unresolved or incomparable;
- whether role vocabularies can remain local at larger scale;
- whether asymmetry improves reader orientation;
- the editorial cost of full traceability;
- scalability across more than two neighborhoods;
- safe limits of tool assistance;
- whether any future comparison warrants an Atlas at all.

## Conclusion

The study identifies a coherent comparison discipline: preserve immutable inputs, compare eligible records dimension by dimension, separate identity from role and structure, audit both directions, retain unequal support, treat asymmetry as evidence, and fail closed when evidence is insufficient. This is a framework proposal, not demonstrated cross-neighborhood performance.
