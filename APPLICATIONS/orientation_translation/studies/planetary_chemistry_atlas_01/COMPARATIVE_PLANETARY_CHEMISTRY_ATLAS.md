# Comparative Planetary Chemistry Atlas

## Planetary Chemistry Atlas 01

- **Status:** non-canonical comparative orientation study
- **Date:** 2026-07-18
- **Input:** supplied orientation sketch (editorial input; repository copy pending)
**Primary question:** Can planetary atmospheres be compared through recurring
compositional and functional patterns without introducing speculative
interpretations?

## Finding

Yes, but only if the comparison is layered. Elements, molecules, atmospheric
mixtures, observed processes, and interpretations are different comparison
objects. A composition does not by itself establish a process, and a shared
molecule does not establish a shared planetary function.

The supplied sketch is useful as an orientation proposal. It is not suitable
as an evidence matrix without revision because it visually turns several
qualified associations into a single causal chain.

## Comparison discipline

```text
element inventory
        ≠
molecular species
        ≠
measured atmospheric mixture
        ≠
observed planetary process
        ≠
functional interpretation
```

Percentages are compared only when they use a compatible basis and reference
region. Cosmic elemental abundance, bulk planetary composition, atmospheric
mixing ratio, and cloud composition remain separate.

## Layer 1 — Chemical elements

| Element | Periodic position | Dominant characteristics relevant here | Atlas boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | Group 1, period 1 | Forms H₂ and many hydrides; low molecular mass matters for escape | Cosmic abundance does not predict an individual atmosphere |
| He | Group 18, period 1 | Noble gas; normally monatomic and weakly reactive | “Does not form molecules” is too absolute |
| C | Group 14, period 2 | Supports CO₂, CO, CH₄ and complex organics | Carbon-bearing species have different chemistry and climate roles |
| N | Group 15, period 2 | N₂ is strongly bonded; nitrogen also enters NH₃ and nitriles | N₂ dominance on Earth and Titan does not imply identical histories |
| O | Group 16, period 2 | Enters H₂O, CO₂, CO, O₂ and mineral oxides | Free O₂ and oxygen bound in molecules must not be conflated |
| S | Group 16, period 3 | Enters SO₂, H₂S and sulfuric-acid aerosols | Sulfur species vary strongly by oxidation state and environment |
| Ar | Group 18, period 3 | Inert tracer and major minor constituent on Earth and Mars | Isotopes and mixing ratios carry different evidence |
| Si | Group 14, period 3 | Major rock-forming element | Usually a surface/interior comparator, not a major atmospheric gas |
| Fe | Group 8, period 4 | Major planetary/interior and mineral constituent | Magnetic field generation cannot be inferred from Fe presence alone |

## Layer 2 — Major atmospheric molecules and species

| Species | Relevant property | Directly observed roles | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| H₂ | Non-polar, very low mass | Dominant gas in giant-planet envelopes | Retention depends on gravity, irradiation, history and temperature |
| He | Monatomic noble gas | Major giant-planet constituent; exospheric constituent elsewhere | Treat as an atom, not a molecule |
| H₂O | Polar; condenses across planetary conditions | Vapor, ice and clouds; liquid cycle on Earth | Presence does not imply surface liquid water |
| CO₂ | Infrared-active; condenses under some Martian conditions | Dominant Venus/Mars gas; greenhouse contribution | Similar mixing ratio does not imply similar surface temperature |
| N₂ | Non-polar; strong triple bond | Dominant Earth/Titan gas | Atmospheric role depends on pressure and coupled chemistry |
| CH₄ | Infrared-active; photochemically reactive | Titan weather/photochemistry; trace or minor giant-planet gas | Biological origin must not be presumed |
| NH₃ | Polar, condensable, chemically reactive | Cloud species and trace gas in giant atmospheres | Abundance is altitude- and model-dependent |
| SO₂ | Reactive sulfur oxide | Venus trace gas/cloud chemistry; volcanic tracer | A trace gas can be important without being compositionally dominant |
| O₂ | Reactive oxidant | Major Earth atmospheric gas | Earth’s abundance is coupled to life and geochemistry; not a generic outcome |
| CO | Reactive carbon oxide | Trace atmospheric constituent on several worlds | Detection alone does not establish a cycle |

## Layer 4 — Observed processes

| Process family | Worlds with clear observations | What is compared |
|---|---|---|
| Dense-atmosphere greenhouse behavior | Venus, Earth; weak greenhouse on Mars | Radiative behavior together with pressure, inventory and clouds |
| Condensation and weather | Earth, Mars, Venus, Titan, giant planets | Different condensates under different pressure-temperature regimes |
| Photochemistry | Venus, Earth, Mars, Titan, giant upper atmospheres | Radiation-driven production and destruction of species |
| Surface–atmosphere exchange | Venus, Earth, Mars, Titan; exosphere-surface exchange on Moon/Mercury | Sources, sinks and cycling, not a single mechanism |
| Atmospheric escape | Observed or constrained across rocky worlds and Titan | Loss processes with gravity, irradiation and composition held explicit |
| Magnetosphere interaction | All scoped worlds, but with very different field geometries | Interaction is observed; “shielding” is not a universal single-variable explanation |
| Internally driven circulation | Jupiter, Saturn, Neptune and also other atmospheres | Solar and internal energy contributions must be separated |

## Layer 5 — Comparative orientation

### Recurring combinations

1. **CO₂-rich rocky atmospheres:** Venus and Mars share a dominant molecule,
   but differ by roughly four orders of magnitude in surface pressure and have
   radically different thermal and hydrological histories.
2. **N₂-rich atmospheres:** Earth and Titan show that the same dominant gas can
   coexist with different secondary gases, temperatures, surfaces and cycles.
3. **H₂/He giant envelopes:** Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants; Uranus and
   Neptune have H₂/He-rich atmospheres above compositionally different deep
   interiors.
4. **Surface-bound exospheres:** Mercury and the Moon demonstrate atmospheric
   states in which particles rarely collide and sources/sinks dominate local
   composition.
5. **Condensable cycles:** H₂O on Earth and CH₄/C₂H₆ on Titan are analogous at
   the level of phase-change-driven landscapes, not chemically identical cycles.

### Outliers

- Earth is the only scoped world with abundant atmospheric O₂ and stable,
  widespread surface liquid water.
- Titan is the only moon in scope with a dense atmosphere and stable surface
  hydrocarbon liquids.
- Venus combines Earth-like size with a very dense CO₂ atmosphere, sulfuric
  acid clouds and extreme surface conditions.
- Neptune’s vigorous weather despite weak sunlight prevents a simple
  solar-input-only ordering of atmospheric activity.

### Missing combinations

These are absences in this corpus, not laws:

- no second Earth-like N₂/O₂ atmosphere;
- no second dense N₂/CH₄ moon atmosphere;
- no H₂/He-dominated terrestrial atmosphere;
- no directly comparable “surface pressure” for the giant planets.

## Special comparative questions

### Why N₂ on Earth and Titan?

The shared observation is robust. A single shared cause is not established by
this atlas. Their different methane, oxygen, temperature, photochemistry,
gravity and source histories must remain visible.

### Why CO₂ on Venus and Mars?

CO₂ dominance is observed on both. The comparison demonstrates that mixing
ratio alone is a poor climate comparator: total atmospheric mass, pressure,
clouds, solar forcing and evolutionary history differ.

### Why do giant planets retain H₂ and He?

High gravity and colder formation/atmospheric environments support retention,
but present composition also reflects formation and evolution. This is a
multi-factor explanation, not a one-variable rule.

### Why has Earth retained surface liquid water?

The observation is secure; the complete causal account is not reducible to one
factor. Pressure-temperature conditions, solar input, atmospheric composition,
geologic cycling and planetary history all participate.

### What governs atmospheric retention?

Current evidence supports a system of factors: gravity/escape velocity,
molecular mass, upper-atmosphere temperature and irradiation, impact history,
geologic supply, chemistry, and non-thermal escape. Magnetic fields affect
solar-wind interaction but must not be treated as a universal on/off shield.
