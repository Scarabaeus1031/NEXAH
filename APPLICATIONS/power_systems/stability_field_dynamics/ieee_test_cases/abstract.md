# Abstract — Stability Field Dynamics in Power Systems

Classical power system stability analysis relies on threshold-based criteria,  
linearization, and system-specific indicators.  
However, these approaches often fail to capture the underlying structural mechanisms  
that govern collapse dynamics.

In this work, we introduce a geometric–dynamical–topological framework  
for analyzing stability in power systems based on phase space structure and flow dynamics.

We show that system evolution is governed by a low-dimensional collapse manifold  
defined by an empirical relation:

d²c ≈ a · c^p · (dc)^q

Across multiple IEEE benchmark systems (9, 14, 30, 57, 118),  
this manifold exhibits stable parameters and consistent structure,  
indicating a universal collapse geometry independent of system size.

We further identify a collapse boundary ("rift") defined by near-zero residuals,  
which acts as a geometric corridor along which systems evolve prior to collapse.  
Stability is then quantified as the distance to this structure,  
providing a continuous and physically interpretable stability metric.

By constructing a vector field representation in phase space,  
we demonstrate that trajectories follow structured flow patterns  
rather than arbitrary paths.  
Collapse emerges when trajectories leave coherent flow regions  
and enter divergence-dominated zones.

Projection into residual–distance space reveals discrete clusters,  
indicating branching behavior and multi-state collapse topology.  
This shows that collapse is not a single deterministic event,  
but a structured transition across multiple possible states.

The resulting framework reduces high-dimensional grid dynamics  
to a small number of universal structural components:

- manifold (geometry)  
- flow (dynamics)  
- rift (boundary)  
- distance (metric)  
- topology (branching)

This enables early detection of instability,  
interpretation of collapse mechanisms,  
and a unified description of stability across systems.
